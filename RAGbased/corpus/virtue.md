# Virtue ethics — character traits and the situations that elicit them

A reference for judging whether a particular character trait is the right one for a given situation. Drawn from Aristotelian virtue ethics and ordinary moral psychology. Paraphrased for retrieval.

## Doctrine of the mean
Most virtues are means between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice (deficient) and recklessness (excessive). The right trait fits the situation; the same disposition that is virtuous in one context can be vicious in another.

## Courage — the right trait for danger and risk
Courage is appropriate when there is real danger or cost in doing the right thing — speaking up against a powerful wrongdoer, intervening to help someone at risk, accepting a hard truth. A situation calling for courage is one where the agent could safely retreat but ought not.

## Honesty — the right trait for testimony and disclosure
Honesty is appropriate when one is asked for the truth, or when withholding it would mislead. Honesty is not the demand to say everything one knows; it is the disposition not to deceive when one is bound to speak truthfully.

## Compassion — the right trait for another's suffering
Compassion is appropriate when one encounters someone in genuine distress and is in a position to attend to it. Sentimentality without action, or cold competence in the face of suffering, both miss the mark.

## Generosity — the right trait for giving
Generosity is appropriate when one has the means to give and someone benefits from receiving. Its excess is wastefulness; its deficiency is stinginess. Generosity is misplaced when it props up a behaviour that should be challenged.

## Patience — the right trait for delay and frustration
Patience is appropriate when waiting is unavoidable or when others need time. Its excess is passivity in the face of injustice; its deficiency is impatience that pressures others unfairly.

## Diligence — the right trait for ongoing tasks
Diligence is appropriate when a task is one's responsibility and requires sustained attention over time. Its deficiency is laziness or neglect; its excess is workaholism that crowds out other duties.

## Humility — the right trait for one's own achievements
Humility is appropriate when one's own success could overshadow others or invite envy. Its excess is self-effacement that hides genuine competence others need; its deficiency is arrogance.

## Loyalty — the right trait for standing relationships
Loyalty is appropriate within a relationship that has earned it: family, long friendship, a team one belongs to. It is wrong to extend loyalty to the point of covering up serious wrongs.

## Justice as a virtue — the right trait for distributing what is shared
The just person gives each their due — not too much, not too little, on grounds the others could accept.

## Temperance — the right trait for appetites
Temperance is appropriate around food, drink, pleasure, and consumption. Its deficiency is intemperance; its excess is asceticism that refuses ordinary goods unnecessarily.

## Curiosity — the right trait for the unknown
Curiosity is appropriate when learning is possible without harm. Its deficiency is incuriosity; its excess is prying — pushing into matters that are not one's business, especially where privacy is at stake.

## Trait–situation fit
A trait described as a virtue does not automatically apply to every situation. Loyalty fits standing relationships, not first encounters. Courage fits danger, not routine compliance. When judging whether a trait describes a person in a situation, ask whether the situation is one that calls for that trait.

## Trait–situation mismatch
A trait is mismatched when the situation does not call for it. Calling someone "brave" for picking the dessert they wanted is mismatched; bravery is not at stake. Calling someone "compassionate" for completing paperwork is mismatched unless the paperwork has a clear connection to another's suffering.

## Vice opposite of virtue
Where a virtue is appropriate, the most informative vices are usually its excess and deficiency, not its mere absence. The opposite of courage is not the absence of fear but cowardice or recklessness, depending on direction.

## Practical wisdom (phronesis)
Knowing which virtue a situation calls for is itself a competence — practical wisdom. The wise agent perceives what the situation needs, what is owed, and what would be excessive or deficient given the agent's role and history.

## Acting from settled character
A virtue is a settled disposition: a brave person tends to act bravely across situations that call for it, not only when convenient. A one-off act does not establish that the agent has the virtue, though it is consistent with having it.

## Cruelty
Cruelty is a vice: the disposition to inflict pain or humiliation for its own sake, or to take pleasure in another's suffering. Acting cruelly is wrong even when the cruelty is small.

## Cowardice and recklessness
Cowardice is the deficiency of courage: avoiding what duty requires because of fear. Recklessness is the excess: courting danger out of disregard for one's own safety or the safety of others.

## Selfishness
Selfishness is the disposition to give one's own interests undue weight. Caring properly for oneself is not selfishness; refusing reasonable demands of others because they are inconvenient is.
