# Deontological principles — Kantian and rule-based ethics

A condensed reference of duty-based moral principles, useful for evaluating whether an excuse, exception, or proposed action respects what a person is owed regardless of consequences. Drawn from Kantian ethics, W. D. Ross's prima facie duties, and standard contractualist sources. Paraphrased for retrieval.

## Categorical imperative — universalizability
Act only according to a maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law. If a proposed reason to act would collapse into incoherence when everyone acted on it (lying promises, free-riding on cooperation), it cannot justify the act.

## Categorical imperative — humanity as an end
Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. Using a person purely as a tool — deceiving them, manipulating them, exploiting their dependence — violates this duty.

## Promise-keeping
A promise creates a duty to perform what was promised, weighty enough that breaking it requires either a comparably weighty competing duty or the consent of the person to whom the promise was made. "I just didn't feel like it" is not a reasonable excuse for breaking a promise.

## Truth-telling
There is a standing duty not to lie. Deception treats others as instruments by depriving them of the information they need to make their own choices. Withholding information is sometimes permissible; actively misleading is harder to justify.

## Reasonable excuses
A reasonable excuse for failing a duty typically requires (a) genuine inability rather than mere unwillingness, (b) a competing duty of at least comparable weight, or (c) the prior consent of the party owed. Excuses of the form "I was busy", "I forgot", or "it benefited me" do not meet this bar for serious duties.

## Unreasonable excuses
An excuse is unreasonable when it offers only the agent's preferences as justification for failing a duty owed to someone else — e.g., refusing to help a roommate move because the agent prefers to relax, refusing to repay a small loan because the agent would rather spend the money.

## Fidelity (Ross)
The duty of fidelity binds us to keep our promises and to honour explicit and implicit commitments, including contractual obligations. Walking away from a signed agreement without notice is a wrong even when no party suffers material loss.

## Reparation (Ross)
The duty of reparation requires us to make up for harms we have caused, even unintentionally. Knocking over a neighbour's mailbox creates a duty to repair or replace it.

## Gratitude (Ross)
The duty of gratitude calls for some return for past benefits voluntarily conferred. Acting toward a benefactor as if they were a stranger when an act of acknowledgement is owed is wrong.

## Beneficence (Ross)
We have a duty to improve the condition of others — to help where we can do so without disproportionate cost. The duty is strongest toward those near, dependent, or in acute need.

## Non-maleficence (Ross)
We have a duty not to injure others. This duty is generally stronger than the duty of beneficence: avoiding harm takes priority over conferring benefit when they conflict.

## Justice (Ross)
We have a duty to ensure that pleasure and pain are distributed in accordance with merit. Punishing someone who did no wrong, or rewarding someone whose claim is weaker than another's, fails this duty.

## Self-improvement (Ross)
We have a duty to improve ourselves morally and intellectually — to develop our capacities. This is a weaker duty than non-maleficence but explains why neglecting one's own development can be a mild moral failing.

## Consent as defeasor
Many prima facie duties to others can be released by the affected person's free and informed consent. A friend who tells you to skip their birthday party releases you from the implicit duty to attend; a stranger has no such standing.

## Duties to family members
Standing relationships generate duties: parents owe care to children, adult children typically owe attention and assistance to elderly parents, partners owe candour and support to one another. These duties are stronger than the equivalents owed to strangers and harder to excuse away.

## Role-based duties
Accepting a role (employee, doctor, teacher, host, guardian) brings with it role-specific duties: the doctor's duty of care, the host's duty of hospitality, the guardian's duty of supervision. Failing the role-duty without a comparably weighty reason is wrong.

## Limits on duties
Duties have limits. A request that would require sacrificing one's own basic well-being, that violates a third party's rights, or that crosses a deep moral line need not be obeyed and can be reasonably refused.
