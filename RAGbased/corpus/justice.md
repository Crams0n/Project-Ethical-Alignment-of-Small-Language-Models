# Justice, fairness, and desert — principles

A reference for evaluating claims about who deserves what, what counts as fair, and when a distribution or judgement is just. Draws on Rawls, Sen, Nozick, and standard fairness principles. Paraphrased for retrieval.

## Formal principle of justice
Treat like cases alike, and different cases differently in proportion to their relevant differences. If two people are similar in all respects relevant to a decision, they should be treated the same.

## Relevant vs irrelevant differences
A difference is relevant to a decision only if it bears on the criterion at stake. Hair colour is irrelevant to a hiring decision for an accountant; accounting skill is relevant. Treating two candidates differently on irrelevant grounds is unjust.

## Desert based on contribution
Other things equal, people who contribute more to a joint endeavour have a stronger claim on its rewards than those who contribute less. "I did most of the work" is, on its face, a reasonable basis for asking for a larger share, though not a decisive one.

## Desert based on need
Some goods (medical care, basic subsistence) are appropriately distributed by need rather than contribution. "I need the medicine more than they do" is a reasonable claim when the goods at stake are tied to basic well-being.

## Equal opportunity
Positions, roles, and prizes that affect life prospects should be open to all with the relevant talents on a fair basis. Excluding someone on the basis of an irrelevant trait — race, gender, family connections — is unjust.

## Rawls' veil of ignorance
A principle of distribution is just if it would be chosen by rational parties who did not yet know which position in society they would occupy. Principles that are only acceptable to those near the top fail this test.

## Difference principle
Inequalities are permissible only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society. A reward scheme that lifts only the already-fortunate is harder to justify than one that lifts the worst-off.

## Procedural justice
A decision is procedurally just when it follows rules that are public, applied consistently, free of bias, open to challenge, and applied by impartial decision-makers. Arbitrary or hidden procedures are unjust even when their outcomes happen to be reasonable.

## Reasonableness of a fairness claim
A complaint of unfairness is reasonable when (a) a relevantly similar case received different treatment, (b) the differential treatment lacks a justifying reason, and (c) the complainant has standing to raise the complaint. Complaints of the form "I expected more" without a comparator, or "I deserved more because I wanted more", are not reasonable.

## Unreasonable claims
A claim is unreasonable when it asks for treatment a relevantly similar person would not receive, or when it ignores the legitimate claims of others. "I should get the promotion because I have been here longest" is reasonable if seniority is the agreed criterion and unreasonable if competence is.

## Punishment proportionality
Punishments and sanctions should be proportionate to the offence. Punishing a minor transgression with a severe sanction is unjust even when the procedure is otherwise fair.

## Compensation for harm
Those harmed have a claim against those who wrongfully harmed them, and against the broader institutions that allowed the harm where applicable. The size of the rightful claim tracks the severity of the harm and the wrongdoer's culpability.

## Equal pay for equal work
People doing the same job, with the same skill and effort, deserve the same compensation. Pay differentials must rest on differences in skill, effort, responsibility, or working conditions, not on the worker's identity.

## Standing
Not everyone has standing to raise every claim. A person who has voluntarily withdrawn from a process, or who never had a stake in it, generally lacks the standing to demand a share of its rewards.

## Sen's capability view
Justice should be assessed in terms of what people are actually able to do and to be, not just the resources they hold. Two people with the same income but different access to education, health, or freedom of movement are not equally well off.

## Reciprocity
Fair cooperation rests on reciprocity: each party benefits, each party contributes, and burdens are shared on terms each could endorse. Free-riding — taking the benefits while shifting burdens onto others — is unjust.

## Children and dependents
Children and other dependents cannot assert their own claims fully. Their interests are nonetheless owed weight, and the failure of an able party to provide for a dependent in their care is a serious injustice.

## Fair recognition
Fair treatment includes recognition: acknowledging contributions, hearing concerns, and treating people as full participants. Persistent failure to recognise someone's contribution is itself an injustice.
