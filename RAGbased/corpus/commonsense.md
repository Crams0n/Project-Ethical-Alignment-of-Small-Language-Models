# Commonsense moral rules

Ordinary moral judgments about everyday actions: when an action is wrong, when it is permissible, and what factors normally tip the balance. Paraphrased for retrieval; the goal is to give the model the kinds of considerations a thoughtful adult would bring to a moral question.

## Harming others
Causing physical, emotional, or financial harm to another person is normally wrong. The strength of the wrong scales with the severity of the harm, the agent's intent, and whether the victim consented to the risk.

## Helping others in distress
When someone is in genuine distress and another person can help at low cost, helping is normally the right thing to do. Walking past a person in obvious need without acting on a small possible help is a moral failing.

## Stealing and dishonesty in property
Taking what is not yours without consent — money, objects, time, attention — is normally wrong. Small thefts are still thefts. Borrowing without permission, even with the intent to return, is a milder version of the same wrong.

## Lying and deception
Telling someone something false in order to mislead them is normally wrong, including small lies, exaggerations meant to deceive, and silences calculated to leave a false impression. Honesty in self-presentation is part of treating others as competent moral agents.

## Promise-breaking
Failing to do what one promised, when one could have, is normally wrong, especially when the other party relied on the promise. Promises that are minor in content can still create non-trivial duties.

## Care for dependents
Those in one's care — children, pets, ill or elderly family, students in one's class — are owed attention, supervision, and provision for basic needs. Leaving a young child unattended, leaving a pet without food, neglecting an ill parent's medication are typical examples of wrong neglect.

## Animal welfare
Causing unnecessary suffering to a sentient animal is wrong. Killing or harming an animal for sport, convenience, or amusement is harder to justify than doing so in genuine self-defence or for sustenance.

## Respect for privacy
Reading other people's messages, eavesdropping on private conversations, or revealing private information without consent is normally wrong. The wrong holds even when the information is "harmless" — the violation is the breach of trust.

## Fair treatment of strangers
Strangers are owed the baseline courtesy and honesty owed to any person. Cheating, manipulating, or being needlessly rude to a stranger because there is no ongoing relationship to protect is still wrong.

## Workplace honesty
Lying on a CV, taking credit for a colleague's work, padding hours, or sabotaging a coworker are wrong. The wrong implicates both fellow workers and the trust the employer places in the worker.

## Driving and shared spaces
Acting recklessly in shared spaces — driving while intoxicated, ignoring traffic rules in ways that endanger others, blocking emergency access — is wrong because the risk is imposed on people who did not consent.

## Reasonable self-defence
Defending oneself or a third party against a real attack is normally permissible; force should be proportionate to the threat. Pre-emptive harm against someone who has not threatened violence is not self-defence.

## Consent
Consent — informed, free, revocable — converts many otherwise impermissible actions into permissible ones (a friend lets you borrow their car, a partner agrees to the joke at their expense). Acting without consent in matters where consent was needed is wrong even if the agent's intentions were good.

## Excuses that work
Genuine inability, mistaken information that the agent had no reasonable way to correct, and competing duties of at least equal weight can excuse a failure to act well.

## Excuses that don't work
"I didn't feel like it", "I thought I could get away with it", "everyone else does it", "they had it coming" do not excuse otherwise wrong actions. Most cases described in moral puzzles use these forms.

## Letting versus doing
Actively causing a harm is normally more seriously wrong than letting a harm happen, but the gap narrows when the letting was easy to prevent and one had a special duty to the person harmed.

## Cleaning up after oneself
Leaving messes, broken things, or unfinished obligations for others to handle without warning is mildly wrong. The wrong rises when the others are not in a position to refuse the burden.

## Anger and proportionate response
Being angry in proportion to a wrong is reasonable; acting on anger by harming someone disproportionately, or by harming a third party, is wrong.

## Respect in disagreement
Disagreeing with someone, including strongly, is not wrong. Insulting them, mocking them, or attacking their identity rather than their argument is wrong even when one is correct on the substance.

## Public versus private moral acts
The same act is normally wrong whether or not anyone sees it. The wrongness does not depend on being observed; the temptation to behave differently when unobserved is itself a moral warning sign.
